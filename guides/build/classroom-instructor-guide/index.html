Teaching with exercises: an instructor's guide

This guide is about the teaching side of Classroom mode: choosing outcomes, writing rubrics, rotating roles, grading a group exercise fairly, adjusting for individual students and keeping student records private. It works for any field the app supports.

Choosing outcomes

Section titled “Choosing outcomes”

A learning outcome says what a student should be able to do after the course. An exercise can show only a few of them, so map each exercise to the outcomes it can really evidence.

  • Write each outcome as something a student does and you can observe: “Decide whether to activate the plan and state the reason” is observable; “Understand the plan” is not.
  • Keep three to six outcomes per exercise. More than that and the rubric turns into a checklist nobody can score during a live session.
  • Give each outcome a Bloom level if your program uses them. Exercises are best at apply, analyze and evaluate; recall belongs in a quiz.
  • Map objectives to outcomes on the Classroom screen. Suggest mappings compares words and proposes pairs; read each one and confirm only the ones that are true. An outcome that no objective evidences shows as not assessed on the dashboard.
  • Save an outcome set as a file to reuse it next term, or to share it with a colleague teaching another section.

Writing rubrics

Section titled “Writing rubrics”
  • One rubric per role, and one for the team. A team rubric scores what no single role owns: coordination, a shared picture of the situation, timely decisions.
  • Each criterion has three to six levels. Describe what you would see at each level, in the same terms, so the step between levels is clear. “Messages were clear” and “Messages were clear and confirmed by the receiver” differ by something a scorer can check.
  • Weight criteria by how much they matter to your outcomes. A criterion with weight 2 counts twice as much in the total.
  • Link each criterion to the outcomes it evidences. The dashboard’s outcome attainment is computed from those links.
  • Print the rubric as a handout and give it to students before the exercise. A student who has seen the rubric can aim at it, and a score they can trace to the rubric is easier to accept.
  • The starter rubrics are original and meant to be edited. Rubrics published by other institutions often carry license terms that do not allow bundling in software; the AAC&U VALUE rubrics, for example, are shared under a non-commercial license. Read the terms of any rubric you import. The AAC&U VALUE initiative page explains how to use them.

Rotating roles

Section titled “Rotating roles”

Students learn a role by playing it, and they learn what others depend on by playing the roles around it. Plan a rotation so that every student plays each required role once over the term.

  • Give the roles and the number of sessions and choose Plan the rotation. Each session covers every role, and no student repeats a role before playing all of them.
  • If you have more students than roles, several students share a role in a session. Make that shared role a small team with one voice.
  • With fewer students than roles, the plan says so. Merge two roles or reduce the roles for that session.
  • Put the quieter roles, observer and evaluator, in the rotation too. Scoring an evaluator on the quality of their observations teaches how evidence works.
  • If a student misses a session, use Create a make-up version. It keeps the objectives, outcomes and rubrics and changes the facts, so the student cannot copy answers.

Grading a group exercise fairly

Section titled “Grading a group exercise fairly”

Group work is hard to grade because one student’s score depends on others. These practices keep it fair.

  • Score from evidence, not from impressions. Have a teaching assistant record observations during the exercise, with the student, the criterion and a note. Count hot wash contributions with the tap button. When you score, the evidence is listed beside each criterion.
  • Treat the suggested level as a prompt to look, not as a score. It follows a fixed rule and shows its basis, but you decide.
  • Use team criteria for team outcomes and individual criteria for individual ones. A student can be set apart from the team score when you have evidence that they did more or less than the team.
  • Run a practice session first. Practice runs are not graded and write nothing to the gradebook, and students get a report afterward that shows what the rubric looks for.
  • For high-stakes assessments, have two evaluators rate the same students. The dashboard shows how often they agreed, and which criteria they disagree on most. Disagreement means the criterion needs a clearer description, not that someone is wrong.
  • Participation shares are an aid for you. A quiet student may have contributed through a decision or a written message; do not turn the share into a grade unless you link it to a criterion you have explained.
  • Small classes give unstable numbers. With a handful of ratings, treat agreement and attainment figures as hints.
  • Let students respond. The score report shows the evidence behind each score; invite students to point out evidence you missed.

Accommodations

Section titled “Accommodations”
  • Record adjustments only, never diagnoses. The record holds a time multiplier, a response method, a packet format, breaks and a quieter role, plus a note only you can see.
  • At session set-up the screen lists the adjustments for the students seated in that session, so none is forgotten.
  • A time multiplier changes that student’s own timed prompts, such as a reflection survey or a drill, on their device. The shared exercise clock and everyone else’s time stay as they are, and nobody sees that the adjustment exists.
  • Apply an approved adjustment as written. Do not extend it to the class and do not narrow it.
  • Offer an equivalent rubric for a quieter role, so the student’s work is judged against criteria of the same weight.

Privacy and FERPA basics

Section titled “Privacy and FERPA basics”

In the United States the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) protects education records: records directly related to a student that an institution maintains. Grades, scores and rubric comments about a named student are education records. Other countries have their own rules; your institution’s policy comes first.

  • The institution holds the records. The app keeps student records in the project folder on your computer and does not send them to any service. Exporting grades is your decision.
  • Share by consent or by role. In general, student information is shared with the student, or with school officials who need it for their work, under your institution’s policy. A report for a student goes to that student.
  • Do not post grades by name or student number. Use your learning management system, which controls who sees what.
  • Keep records in the right place. The project folder holds student names. Do not copy it to a shared drive, a public repository or an email. The folder named classroom inside it is left out of copies made with Duplicate project, of exports and of packs, but a copy of the whole folder made by hand takes it along.
  • Models never see student records by default. Anything sent to a cloud model is shown to you first for review and can be redacted; a reflection from a student is never sent without that preview. Scores are never decided by a model.
  • Delete when you are done. Delete one student, or the whole term, from the Classroom screen. Deleting removes the roster entry, evidence, scores, reflections and accommodations together.
  • Keep to what you need. Add a student id only if your gradebook needs it to match rows.

Where to find teaching material

Section titled “Where to find teaching material”

The app does not bundle teaching documents whose terms you must check; it points to them. Documents produced by the United States government are generally in the public domain, but a page can include material from others, so read the notice on each.

  • HSEEP, the Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program: the method the exercise documents follow.
  • FEMA Emergency Management Institute independent study courses: free courses, useful as pre-reading.
  • CISA tabletop exercise packages: ready-made cybersecurity scenarios.

Import any of these into a project as documents, and the app grounds the exercise in them.

Example course plans

Section titled “Example course plans”

These outlines show how the pieces fit. Each can be built with the starter rubrics.

  • An emergency management course, four exercises. Rotate four roles over four sessions (incident commander, public information officer, operations, evaluator). Score the player-role rubric for the three players and the evaluator rubric for the fourth, and the team rubric for all. Run session one as a practice run.
  • A cybersecurity incident response course. Students rotate through incident commander, communications lead and analyst, and one student each session acts as evaluator, graded on observation quality. Two evaluators rate the same player on communication so you can see how consistent the rubric is.
  • A journalism verification lab. Add a crisis communications toolkit to the scenario if your installed packs have one. Students play editor, reporter and verifier; the team rubric scores whether the story went out only after the facts were confirmed.
